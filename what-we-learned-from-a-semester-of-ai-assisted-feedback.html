What We Learned From a Semester of AI-Assisted Feedback

A trial across two faculties tested whether AI-generated draft feedback could speed up marking without flattening it into generic comments.

By the Learning & Teaching Office 4 min read
Image goes here
images/placeholder-ai-assisted-feedback.jpg

For one semester, a small group of lecturers across the Faculty of Business and Economic Sciences and the Faculty of Engineering, the Built Environment and Technology piloted a workflow in which an AI tool generated a first draft of feedback on student assignments, which lecturers then reviewed, edited and released under their own name. The aim was narrow and practical: could a first-pass draft meaningfully cut down marking time without students receiving feedback that reads as generic or impersonal.

Twelve lecturers and just over 600 student assignments were involved across the pilot, spanning first-year and second-year modules. Lecturers uploaded a marking rubric and a sample of "gold standard" comments at the start, which the tool used to generate draft feedback aligned to their own marking style rather than a one-size-fits-all template.

Where it helped

The clearest win was on high-volume, formative assessments — short weekly tasks and draft submissions — where feedback often gets thin simply because of time pressure. Lecturers in the pilot reported spending noticeably less time on these lower-stakes tasks, and several said the quality of feedback on them actually improved, because the draft gave them a starting point to sharpen rather than a blank page to fill under time pressure.

Turnaround time was the other headline result: draft-assisted feedback on formative tasks was returned to students a median of four days faster than the previous semester's benchmark, which matters most for feedback students might actually act on before the next related assessment is due.

Where it didn't

The picture was more mixed for high-stakes, substantial assignments — essays, reports, design projects — where lecturers said editing a draft sometimes took as long as writing comments from scratch, particularly where a student's work was unusual or didn't fit the patterns the tool had been trained on. A few lecturers also flagged a subtler risk: a tendency for draft feedback to smooth over genuinely weak or confused writing with polished-sounding generic comments, requiring active vigilance to catch.

"It's not a replacement for reading the work closely — if anything it demands more attention at the editing stage, because a bad edit is worse than no draft at all. But for the routine stuff, it gave me back real hours." — Pilot participant, Faculty of Business and Economic Sciences

Based on the pilot's findings, the tool will be made available more broadly for formative assessment use from the next academic year, with guidance materials on where it adds value and where it doesn't.
