Rethinking Assessment: A Guide to Authentic Tasks

A practical framework for replacing high-stakes exams with assessments that mirror real professional practice, developed with input from five faculties.

By the Learning & Teaching Office 19 Aug 2026

A new framework for assessment redesign, developed with input from five faculties over the past two semesters, sets out a practical approach to replacing high-stakes, closed-book exams with tasks that more closely resemble the kind of work graduates will actually be asked to do. The guide doesn't argue for abolishing exams outright, but makes the case that they are frequently used as a default rather than a deliberate choice.

At its core is a simple test the framework asks lecturers to apply to any assessment: would a competent professional in this field ever be asked to do this task, in this way, under these conditions? Where the answer is no, the guide walks through alternatives — case analyses, client briefs, structured problem sets with open resources, oral defences of written work — matched to different disciplines and class sizes.

Work on the framework began after informal conversations at a previous symposium revealed just how much duplicated effort was happening in isolation — multiple departments independently reinventing similar authentic assessment formats without knowing colleagues elsewhere had already worked through the same design problems. Consolidating that scattered practice into a single shared guide became the project's real starting point.

Five faculties, five different starting points

The framework's development leaned heavily on input from academic staff already experimenting with authentic assessment in isolation, often without knowing colleagues elsewhere in the university were doing the same. Contributors from Engineering brought design-brief formats already common in that discipline; contributors from Health Sciences brought structured clinical scenarios; Humanities colleagues contributed portfolio-based approaches built around sustained argument rather than single high-stakes submissions.

What emerged wasn't a single template but a shared vocabulary and a set of worked examples per faculty, published alongside the guide, so lecturers adapting the framework in their own modules have a concrete starting point rather than a blank page.

The moderation question

Authentic tasks raise a fair question that the guide addresses directly: are they harder to mark consistently across a large cohort? The framework's answer is a heavier upfront investment in detailed, criterion-based rubrics, co-developed by teaching teams rather than written solo, paired with structured moderation sessions before marks are finalised.

"The exam was easy to mark and hard to defend as actually testing what we cared about. This took more work to set up, but the rubric conversations alone taught my teaching team more about what we were really assessing than years of exams had." — Contributing lecturer, Faculty of Engineering, the Built Environment and Technology

The guide is available through the Learning and Teaching Office, with faculty-specific consultation sessions being scheduled for departments wanting support applying it to their own modules.
