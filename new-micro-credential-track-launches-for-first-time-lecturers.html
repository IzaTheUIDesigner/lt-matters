New Micro-Credential Track Launches for First-Time Lecturers

A stackable, credit-bearing pathway aims to get new academics teaching-ready within their first eighteen months on staff.

By the Learning & Teaching Office 3 min read

Every year, a portion of the university's newest academic appointments arrive with deep disciplinary expertise and little to no formal preparation for standing in front of a class. The Staff Development unit has built a response: a micro-credential track aimed squarely at first-time lecturers, structured to be completed alongside a normal teaching load rather than instead of one.

The track is made up of three short, credit-bearing modules — Foundations of Course Design, Assessment That Aligns, and Facilitating Discussion — each running over four weeks with a mix of online content and two in-person workshops. Completing all three earns a formal micro-credential that is recorded on the staff member's professional development record, and can be built toward a full postgraduate teaching qualification later if they choose to continue.

The three modules are taught by a mix of Learning and Teaching Office staff and academic staff already identified as strong classroom practitioners in their own departments, deliberately avoiding a model where "how to teach" is delivered only by people who don't currently teach undergraduates themselves. Coordinators say this cross-appointment approach took longer to set up than a conventional training format would have, but has consistently been flagged by participants as what makes the sessions feel credible rather than theoretical.

Built around the first eighteen months

The timing is deliberate. Programme coordinators structured the track around what they describe as the "steepest part of the curve" — the first three to four semesters, when new lecturers are simultaneously learning their content, their students and their institution. Enrolment is capped at 25 per cohort to keep the workshop sessions discussion-based rather than lecture-style, and two cohorts will run in the second half of 2026, with a third planned for early 2027.

Unlike some previous staff development offerings, the track carries built-in accountability: participants submit a short teaching artefact — a rubric, a lesson plan, a sample assessment — after each module, reviewed by a peer facilitator rather than marked formally. Early feedback from the pilot cohort suggests this lower-stakes review process is what's keeping engagement high through to completion.

What lecturers say

For many participants, the value has been less about new theory and more about permission to ask basic questions in a room full of people asking the same ones.

"I'd been teaching for two semesters before this started, mostly making it up as I went. Having a structured space to workshop an actual rubric I was going to use the following week — that was worth more than any generic training I'd sat through before." — Participant, Faculty of Science

Applications for the next cohort open in September, with priority given to academic staff in their first two years at the university.
