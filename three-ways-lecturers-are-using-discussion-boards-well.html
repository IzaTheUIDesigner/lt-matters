Three Ways Lecturers Are Using Discussion Boards Well

Three simple habits are turning quiet discussion boards into some of the most active spaces in the LMS.

By the Learning & Teaching Office 18 Aug 2026

Discussion boards have a reputation, not entirely undeserved, as one of the quietest corners of the learning management system — a required feature that students visit once to post something and never return to. But a small number of modules across the university are seeing genuinely active, sustained discussion, and a look at what those lecturers are doing differently points to three habits rather than any single trick.

The pattern holds across disciplines as different as philosophy and structural engineering, suggesting the technique matters more than the subject matter.

Asking questions with no single right answer

The single biggest factor separating active boards from quiet ones is the type of question being asked. Boards built around questions with a clear correct answer tend to get a handful of posts and stop — once someone's posted the right answer, there's little reason for anyone else to add anything. Boards built around genuinely open questions, where a well-reasoned wrong answer is still worth engaging with, tend to keep generating responses for days.

Lecturers using this approach well tend to post questions that ask students to take a position and defend it, rather than questions that ask students to identify a fact.

Showing up as the lecturer, briefly

The second habit is presence without dominance. Lecturers running active boards tend to check in once or twice over the life of a discussion — often just to highlight two or three particularly interesting student posts by name, or to gently push back on a common misconception — rather than posting a lengthy response to every thread, which several lecturers said tends to have a chilling effect on further student contributions.

The third habit, closely related, is keeping the window open long enough for genuine back-and-forth to develop — boards that open and close within 48 hours rarely develop into real discussion, where boards left open for a week or more give students time to return, read what others have said, and respond to each other rather than just to the original prompt.

"I stopped grading discussion posts individually and started just asking a real question I was curious about. Participation went up and the quality of the arguments went up with it." — Lecturer, Faculty of Law

None of the three habits require new tools or additional workload — mostly, lecturers running successful boards describe it as a shift in what they're asking for, not how much time they're spending.
