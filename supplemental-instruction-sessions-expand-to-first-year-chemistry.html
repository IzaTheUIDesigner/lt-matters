Supplemental Instruction Sessions Expand to First-Year Chemistry

Peer-led study sessions, shown to improve pass rates in first-year mathematics, are now being piloted in chemistry ahead of a full rollout in 2027.

By the Learning & Teaching Office 10 Aug 2026
Image goes here
images/story-6.jpg

Peer-led supplemental instruction sessions, credited with improving pass rates in first-year mathematics over the past several years, are now being piloted in first-year chemistry, with a view to a full rollout in 2027 if the pilot performs as expected. The format is unchanged from mathematics: weekly, informal study sessions led not by academic staff but by students who passed the same module well in a previous year.

Supplemental instruction sessions are deliberately kept separate from formal tutorials. Where a tutorial is typically run by a postgraduate tutor working through set problems, an SI session is led by a peer close enough in experience to remember exactly which parts of the module were confusing, and structured around questions the group itself raises rather than a fixed script.

The mathematics programme that inspired this expansion has now run for four consecutive years, with pass rates in the supported module rising each year since its introduction and attendance at SI sessions consistently tracking above 60 percent of enrolled students by the midpoint of a typical semester. Those figures formed the basis of the case made to extend the model into a second discipline.

Recruiting and training peer leaders

Peer leaders for the chemistry pilot were recruited from students who completed the module in the top band the previous year, and went through a two-day training programme before the semester began, covering facilitation techniques rather than content review — the assumption being that a strong pass already demonstrates content mastery, and the skill that needs building is drawing other students into productive discussion.

Six sessions are running weekly across the pilot, each capped at around 20 students to keep the format conversational rather than lecture-style. Attendance is voluntary and unassessed, which organisers say is central to the model — students who attend are there because they've decided the session is useful to them, not because it's compulsory.

Early signs from the pilot

It's still early in the chemistry pilot, but attendance patterns are tracking close to what mathematics saw in its own first pilot semester, with students who attend regularly reporting more confidence going into tests, even before final marks are available to confirm the pattern holds.

"In the lecture I'm too embarrassed to ask what I think is a stupid question in front of 300 people. In SI, someone else in the room usually has the same question, and the peer leader's been exactly where we are." — First-year chemistry student

A decision on expanding the programme university-wide is expected once full-semester results from the chemistry pilot are reviewed early next year.
