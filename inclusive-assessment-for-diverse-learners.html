Inclusive Assessment for Diverse Learners

The final session in the series, on 24 September, looks at adapting assessment formats for a more diverse student body.

By the Learning & Teaching Office 24 Sept 2026
Image goes here
images/placeholder-inclusive-assessment.jpg

The final session in the Learning and Teaching Office's September workshop series, Inclusive Assessment for Diverse Learners, running on 24 September, looks at how assessment formats themselves can unintentionally disadvantage students depending on language background, disability, or prior educational preparation — and what practical adjustments address this without lowering the bar being assessed against.

The session opens from a simple premise: an assessment is meant to test whether a student has met a learning outcome, not how quickly they can read dense instructions under time pressure, or how comfortable they are with a single rigid format. Where those two things get conflated, the workshop argues, assessments end up measuring something other than what they were designed to measure.

The session draws on consultation with the university's Disability Unit and with academic staff who teach modules with a high proportion of English additional-language students, aiming to ground the workshop's recommendations in patterns actually observed across the university's own classrooms rather than in general accessibility literature alone.

Beyond formal accommodations

Much of the session moves past the university's existing formal accommodation processes — which cover a defined set of registered needs — to look at universal design choices that benefit a much broader group of students by default, rather than requiring individual students to seek out an exception. Examples covered include offering a choice of assessment format for the same learning outcome, using plain-language instructions reviewed for clarity before an assessment goes live, and building in structured time buffers for tasks with a strong language-processing component.

Facilitators are explicit that this isn't about making assessments easier. The distinction drawn throughout the session is between the difficulty of the content being tested, which shouldn't change, and the difficulty of accessing the assessment itself, which often can be reduced without affecting rigour at all.

Practical changes, not policy rewrites

Rather than proposing large policy changes, the workshop focuses on small, immediately applicable adjustments lecturers can make to their next assessment — the kind of changes that don't require faculty-level sign-off or additional resourcing.

"I expected a session about compliance and accommodation forms. It was actually about rewriting instructions so more of my class understood what I was asking the first time they read it." — Attendee, previous inclusive assessment session

The session runs on 24 September, closing out the four-part assessment design series that began in September with sessions on outcomes alignment, rubric design and authentic task design.
