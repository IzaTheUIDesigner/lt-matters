Designing Rubrics That Actually Save You Time

A hands-on session on 3 September tackles the rubric problem: detailed enough to be fair, quick enough to actually use.

By the Learning & Teaching Office 3 Sept 2026
Image goes here
images/placeholder-designing-rubrics.jpg

Every lecturer who has ever built a rubric knows the tension at its centre: detailed enough to mark fairly and defend the mark if challenged, but quick enough to actually use across a stack of forty, eighty or two hundred scripts without the rubric itself becoming the bottleneck. The 3 September workshop, Designing Rubrics That Actually Save You Time, is built entirely around resolving that tension rather than treating it as unavoidable.

The session opens with a short diagnostic exercise: participants bring a rubric they currently use and time themselves marking two or three sample scripts against it, flagging exactly where they slow down or have to stop and think. Almost without exception, organisers say, the slowdowns cluster around the same two or three criteria — usually the vaguest ones on the page.

The workshop caps attendance at 20 participants specifically so every attendee gets individual feedback on their own rubric during the diagnostic exercise, rather than working through generic examples alone. Facilitators say this hands-on ratio is the main reason the session runs longer and smaller than most standard staff development workshops, and also why places tend to fill quickly once registration opens.

Fixing the vague criteria, not adding more

Where many rubric-building exercises push participants toward adding more criteria for precision, this workshop takes the opposite approach: identifying which existing criteria are actually doing the work of distinguishing a strong answer from a weak one, and cutting or merging the rest. Facilitators argue that most rubrics fail not because they're too short, but because they're padded with criteria that sound rigorous without actually changing where a mark lands.

Participants leave the session with a working draft of a revised rubric for the assessment they brought, along with a simple test to apply to any future rubric before finalising it: could two different markers, using this rubric independently on the same script, land within a mark or two of each other without discussion.

Built from real marking pain

The workshop grew out of complaints, common across faculties, that moderation meetings routinely surface wide disagreement between markers using rubrics everyone thought were clear until they were actually tested against real scripts side by side.

"I went from a two-page rubric nobody actually followed to one page that two of us could apply the same way. Moderation went from an hour of arguing to fifteen minutes." — Attendee, previous rubric workshop

The session runs on 3 September as part of the Learning and Teaching Office's assessment design workshop series, with places limited to keep the diagnostic exercise genuinely hands-on.
