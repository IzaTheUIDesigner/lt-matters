Facilitating Large Classes Without Losing the Room

This 10 September session shares practical techniques for keeping large lecture halls engaged without extra equipment or staff.

By the Learning & Teaching Office 10 Sept 2026
Image goes here
images/placeholder-facilitating-large-classes.jpg

With a growing number of modules across the university now running with enrolments well past 200 students, the 10 September workshop, Facilitating Large Classes Without Losing the Room, addresses one of the most consistently requested topics from recent staff development feedback: how to keep a genuinely large class engaged without additional staff, equipment or budget.

The session is built around techniques that scale, deliberately excluding approaches that work well in small classes but fall apart past a certain size — cold-calling individual students, for instance, or open-floor discussion, both of which tend to engage a small vocal minority in a large room while the rest disengage entirely.

The workshop draws on classroom observations gathered from six large modules across three faculties over the past year, with facilitators sitting in on lectures ranging from 150 to over 400 students to identify which engagement techniques actually held up as class size grew, rather than relying solely on general literature about large-class teaching.

Techniques that work at scale

In their place, the workshop covers structured techniques designed for rooms of any size: think-pair-share exercises with strict time limits, low-tech polling using coloured cards or a show of hands rather than clicker systems, and short, frequent low-stakes questions built directly into the lecture rather than saved for the end. Facilitators emphasise that the goal isn't constant activity, but predictable rhythm — students in a large class who know roughly when they'll be asked to actively participate stay more consistently attentive than students waiting to see if today is a passive day or not.

A second strand of the workshop addresses the physical and acoustic realities of large venues directly — where to stand, how to use silence rather than raising your voice to regain attention, and simple changes to slide density that make content easier to follow from the back rows.

Grounded in the university's own large classes

Rather than drawing on generic large-class literature alone, the session's facilitators have spent time observing large modules across the university's own faculties, and several of the techniques covered were drawn directly from lecturers already running large classes well.

"I used to dread my 300-person Monday lecture. A few small changes — mostly about rhythm, not content — and it stopped feeling like performing into a void." — Lecturer, previous large-class workshop attendee

The session runs on 10 September and is open to academic staff teaching modules of any size, though it is aimed particularly at those managing enrolments above 150 students.
