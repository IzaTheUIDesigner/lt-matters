Flipped Classrooms Take Root Across Three Faculties

Three faculties have quietly shifted lecture content online and reserved contact time for problem-solving — and the approach is starting to spread organically.

By the Learning & Teaching Office Most Read This Issue
Image goes here
images/placeholder-flipped-classrooms.jpg

A growing number of lecturers across three faculties — Science, Engineering the Built Environment and Technology, and Business and Economic Sciences — have independently arrived at a similar redesign over the past two years: moving core content delivery online, in the form of short recorded videos or structured readings, and reclaiming scheduled class time for active problem-solving and discussion.

None of the three faculties coordinated the shift centrally. Instead, the approach appears to have spread the way effective teaching practices often do — one lecturer trying it, talking about the results with colleagues, and a handful of others adapting the same basic structure to their own modules.

No formal count of how many modules have adopted the format exists yet, precisely because the shift has been so decentralised, but informal estimates from department heads across the three faculties put the number at somewhere between fifteen and twenty modules currently running some version of the flipped structure, most of them large first- and second-year courses.

The same shift, three different starting points

In Science, the shift began in a large first-year physics module where lecturers found themselves repeating the same explanations year after year in the same order, regardless of where a given cohort was actually struggling. Moving that explanation online, available on demand, freed contact time for the actual sticking points a specific cohort was hitting in a given week.

In Engineering, the driver was slightly different — a recognition that design and problem-solving skills are best built through supervised practice, not observed through a lecture, which made contact time far better spent on guided problem sets than on content transmission. Business and Economic Sciences arrived at a similar structure somewhat later, largely following case studies from the other two faculties.

What's making it stick

Lecturers who've made the switch describe an adjustment period — students accustomed to a traditional lecture format sometimes resist the shift initially, expecting to be walked through content passively rather than arriving having already engaged with it. Several lecturers said the format only really works once students trust that the in-class time will be worth having prepared for, which takes a few weeks of consistent delivery to establish.

"The first few weeks, half the room hadn't watched the videos and class time was a mess. By week six, most had figured out the videos were the only way the class actually made sense, and it clicked." — Lecturer, Faculty of Science

With the approach now established independently across three faculties, the Learning and Teaching Office is exploring whether a shared set of guidelines and support resources would help lecturers elsewhere adopt the format without each having to work out the early adjustment period from scratch.
