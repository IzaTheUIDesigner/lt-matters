Two Lecturers Recognised for Excellence in Teaching

This year's Teaching Excellence Awards celebrate innovative practice in first-year mathematics and clinical nursing education.

By the Learning & Teaching Office 17 Aug 2026

This year's Teaching Excellence Awards recognised two lecturers whose work, on the surface, could hardly look more different — one redesigning how first-year mathematics is taught to large, mixed-preparedness cohorts, the other rebuilding clinical simulation training for nursing students. What the judging panel singled out in both cases was the same underlying quality: a willingness to rebuild an established course from the ground up rather than adjust it at the margins.

The mathematics award recognised a multi-year overhaul of the first-year calculus sequence, which had for years carried one of the highest failure rates in the faculty. Rather than slow the pace or lower the bar, the redesign restructured tutorials around small, mixed-ability problem-solving groups and introduced weekly low-stakes diagnostic quizzes that let both students and lecturers see gaps early, well before the first major test.

The Teaching Excellence Awards, now in their eighth year, are judged by a panel of senior academics and past recipients, drawing on student evaluations, peer nominations and a portfolio submitted by each nominee. This year's panel noted an unusually strong field, with both winning submissions standing out for evidence of sustained, multi-year impact rather than a single well-received semester.

Rebuilding calculus from the tutorial up

Pass rates in the module have risen for three consecutive years since the redesign began, though the recipient was quick to credit the tutors who deliver the small-group sessions as much as the structural changes themselves. "The framework only works if the people in the room running it believe in it," they noted at the ceremony.

Simulation training that mirrors the ward

The nursing education award went to a lecturer who rebuilt the department's clinical simulation curriculum to more closely mirror the unpredictability of an actual ward — introducing scenario branches where a simulated patient's condition changes mid-exercise depending on decisions students make, rather than following a fixed script. Students now debrief in structured small groups immediately after each simulation, walking through their reasoning in real time while it's still fresh.

Colleagues nominating the recipient pointed to measurably higher confidence scores among students entering their first clinical placements since the new format was introduced, alongside qualitative feedback describing the simulations as "the closest thing to the real ward before the real ward."

"You can't teach someone to think on their feet with a script that never changes. The moment we let the scenario respond to what students actually did, the whole exercise became worth ten times as much." — Award recipient, Department of Nursing Science

Both recipients will present their approaches at a Learning and Teaching Office showcase later this year, open to colleagues across faculties looking to adapt similar strategies in their own courses.
